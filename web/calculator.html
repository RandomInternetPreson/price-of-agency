Who holds the eraser?

A ruler for your schedule.

“Every plan is written in pencil, and the eraser belongs to the manager.” That is from The Price of Agency, Chapter 7. This page asks nine questions about one job. It tells you how often that job’s schedule lets you be where you planned to be: at dinner with your family, at your child’s game, at a class. Then it puts ten thousand people on the same schedule and shows what it does to them, and which change would help them most. Its counts are the most a schedule allows. It says what they leave out.

Nothing you type leaves this page. There is no account, no server and no counter. The page is one file; a line near the top of it tells your browser to refuse every network connection. Save the file, turn the network off, and it works the same.

How far two weeks’ notice reaches

Seattle’s law asks for two weeks’ notice. The laws like it in other places ask for the same, or less. Two weeks is a floor, and this table shows what stands on it and what does not. A fixed schedule means the same hours every week, known for as long as you hold the job.

The book’s own data points the same way (Chapter 13). In the Federal Reserve’s survey of households, workers whose employer sets their hours and tells them three weeks ahead or more look like workers on fixed hours when asked about money. Workers told later than that, two weeks included, do not.

        What these counts take for granted

        Part two counts one thing: whether a person gets to every class with enough left to learn in them. The ten thousand people in it have been handed everything else. Each of them found a course that teaches what they need. Each of them can pay for it. Nobody at home needs those evenings more than the course does. No number on this page checks any of that, and wherever any of it fails, every count in the table is lower.

        So read each count as the most this job allows, and not as what happens. A row that reads 10,000 says the job is no longer what stands in the way. It does not say that ten thousand people have what they need. And two weeks’ notice is where the laws stop. It is not where a life stops, which is why the table has a row for a fixed schedule.

        The same groups on the book’s two rulers EXACT

        The book measures freedom with two rulers. Independence asks how hard it is to guess what a person will do just from the situation they are in. Aim asks how much what they do has to do with what happens next. Each numbered point is the whole group of ten thousand under one schedule. Further right is more independence. Further up is more aim.

        § 3What this is

        What this page counts, and what it cannot see

        What it cannot see. Most of what makes a life is not a course or an appointment. It is dinner with the people you love, a child’s first steps, a board game at the family table, an evening with a pet and a film. Some of it is not a plan at all: a child needs someone there most evenings for years, and a birth comes when it comes. This page cannot measure any of that and does not try to. It counts one thing about those hours: whether they are still yours when they come.

        Part one is your own numbers. How often you can be where you planned to be is arithmetic on your answers. It holds for a family dinner exactly as it holds for a class. The suppositions it makes are listed at the bottom of the page.

        Part two is the book’s model, and it is narrower. It is about learning, because that is what the book’s theorem is about. Learning, in the book, is whatever leaves a person knowing something their surroundings do not tell them: a class, a trade, an hour alone with a problem, years among the people they live with. The model does not ask where the knowledge came from, only how often it is right. This page uses a course because classes can be counted, and it cannot count the rest. In the model, each class attended adds a little knowledge. Until there is enough, a person does what the situation in front of them calls for, the same as before. Once there is enough, what they learned starts to change what they do. The book calls that point the gate (Chapters 5 and 7). Nobody has measured where the gate is for a real person, in classes or in hours, and the book says so in Chapter 15. What the model gives is the shape: nothing, nothing, and then something. A schedule decides how many people get that far. To the model this page adds one rule of its own: a course counts only when every class was attended, because that is who gets the certificate.

        Why ten thousand people. One person’s year is one roll of the dice. What a schedule does shows only across many people living on it. The ten thousand are not you, and no dot is a forecast about you.

        Who changed it. Who asked for a change is the wrong test, and this page does not use it. A worker asks for a change because an earlier change broke her childcare. She asks for more shifts because the week came out short. She is asked to volunteer. And sometimes a manager moves a shift to exactly where she wanted it. The book’s test is different (Chapters 7 and 13): could you have left things as they were at no cost to you, and did the change leave you better or worse off? Question 2 counts a change when the decision was not freely yours, whoever spoke first.

        The five questions the book leaves with every reader:

        1. Who holds the eraser? Not whether the hours vary: who can change them, and on how much notice.
        2. What does one mistake cost here?
        3. One ruler, or two? Unpredictable is not free, and orderly is not fine: ask whose aim the hours serve.
        4. How many heads were there, really?
        5. Which tally, and which score?
        How each number is computed

        How often you can be there. A plan made h days ahead holds unless one of two things happens. (1) If h is more days than you know your hours ahead (question 1), the plan was made without knowing your shifts, and it is taken when a shift lands on it. The chance of that is the share of days you work, times the share of a sixteen-hour day that a shift plus a two-hour plan cover. (2) Your hours for that day get changed, by a decision not freely yours, with less than h days’ warning. The chance of that is your answer to question 2, times the share of changes that come with less than h days’ warning, taking the warnings to be spread around your answer to question 3 (exponentially). The page supposes you cannot get a day off by asking for it. Where an employer gives days off when asked and keeps to it, a plan made far ahead holds more often than this. Only the schedule takes plans in this count. Illness, a bus that does not come and everything else that costs an evening are left out, under every schedule alike.

        “Two weeks’ notice, kept.” Hours known fourteen days ahead, and no change made inside those fourteen days. Changes that come with more warning than that still happen as often as before.

        “A fixed schedule.” The same hours every week, known for as long as the job is held, and not changed. The page counts every plan as holding, because the hours are known when the plan is made and can be planned around. It does not count whether an employer gives time off.

        Three days in a row. Each of the three days has to be free for the whole day, so the chance that a shift lands on a day is the share of days you work. All three have to hold, each on its own chance.

        One plan a week for a year. Each of ten thousand people makes 52 plans, and each plan holds with the chance above, independently.

        The model in part two. A future with four possibilities. The situation points to the right one half the time and otherwise points at random. Each class attended is a glimpse that points to the right one with probability 0.35 and otherwise at random. The person weighs everything exactly and chooses what costs least on average: doing what the situation says and being wrong costs 1, going their own way and being wrong costs the answer to question 7. These are the constants of the book’s Theorem 16 (code/iterative_time_T16.py) and the costs of its Theorem 5b (code/mistake_budget_T18.py). code/calculator_model.py in the book’s repository computes the same tables in Python and checks them against both.

        Enough learning. The smallest number of classes attended at which the person ever goes their own way. For a person who comes to class with as much left as on a day off: two when a mistake costs the same either way, three when it costs twice as much, four when it costs five or ten times as much.

        Question 9. “About half as much” halves what one class teaches: each class points to the right possibility with probability 0.175, and the four numbers become three, five, six and seven. “Very little” quarters it, to 0.0875, and they become five, eight, eleven and thirteen. That a tired person learns less from a class in exact proportion is this page’s supposition, not a measurement.

        The course. One class a week, the times set on the day of signing up, so class number j is a plan made j weeks ahead. Each person attends each class with the chance above for a plan made that far ahead, independently. A person who attends every class has finished. Afterwards that person meets one decision, with everything the course taught. A person who missed a class has not finished, and meets the same decision as if there had been no course: no certificate, and nothing an employer will take their word for. That a course counts only when it is finished is this page’s supposition about courses that end in a certificate. It is not part of the book’s model, in which every class attended adds something. The same ten thousand people, with the same luck, are shown under every schedule, so the difference between two pictures is the schedule and nothing else. Counts of people come from the simulation. The two rulers are computed exactly for the whole group.

        Three days in a row, ten thousand people. Each person needs three whole days, three months ahead, and each day holds on its own chance.

        Seattle’s law. The Secure Scheduling Ordinance, as Seattle’s Office of Labor Standards states it: for a change made less than fourteen days before the shift, one hour of pay when hours are added or the time is moved with no loss of hours, and half of the hours not worked when hours are cut. It covers retail and food-service chains with five hundred or more employees. It does not pay for a change the worker requested in writing or a swap between workers.
